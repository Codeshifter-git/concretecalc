How it works

How to calculate concrete:
five steps, no guesswork.

Concrete is bought by volume. Measure each part, turn it into cubic feet, add a little extra for waste, and order by the cubic yard.

The formulas the calculator usesSlab / footing: length × width × thicknessRound column / pad: π × radius² × heightWall: (length × height − openings) × thicknessStairs: width × tread × riser × n(n+1) ÷ 2One rule: convert every dimension to the same unit before you multiply. Thickness in inches is the usual trap — divide it by 12 to get feet.
01

Measure the pour

Length and width in feet, thickness in inches. For a round pour, measure the diameter and the depth. Measure twice — a wrong number here is a wrong order.

10 ft × 10 ft × 4 in
02

Turn inches into feet

Divide the thickness by 12. Four inches is a third of a foot — 0.333 ft. This is the step most people skip, and it is the one that breaks the math.

4 in ÷ 12 = 0.333 ft
03

Multiply to cubic feet

Length × width × thickness, all in feet. A 10 by 10 foot slab at 4 inches is 33.33 cubic feet.

10 × 10 × 0.333 = 33.33 ft³
04

Divide by 27 for cubic yards

One cubic yard is 27 cubic feet. Divide your cubic feet by 27 and you have the yards to order.

33.33 ft³ ÷ 27 = 1.23 yd³
05

Add waste, then order

Never order the exact number. Add 5–10% for a clean pour, 10–15% for rough ground. Then round up — a second truck costs far more than a little extra concrete.

1.23 yd³ × 1.10 = 1.36 yd³ to order

Task 1: How much for a 12 × 12 ft patio, 4 in thick? +

A patio is 12 feet long and 12 feet wide, poured 4 inches thick. How many cubic yards is that?

Answer: 4 in ÷ 12 = 0.333 ft. 12 × 12 × 0.333 = 48 ft³. 48 ÷ 27 = 1.78 yd³. With 10% waste, order 1.96 yd³.

Task 2: How much for a 60 ft footing, 12 in wide, 8 in deep? +

A footing runs 60 feet, 12 inches wide and 8 inches deep. How many cubic yards is that?

Answer: 60 × 1 × 0.667 = 40 ft³. 40 ÷ 27 = 1.48 yd³. With 10% waste, order 1.63 yd³.

Questions
people ask.

How do I calculate concrete for a slab? +

Multiply length × width × thickness, with every dimension in the same unit first. A 10 foot by 10 foot slab, 4 inches thick, is 10 × 10 × 0.333 = 33.33 cubic feet, or 1.23 cubic yards. The calculator above does the unit math for you.

How much concrete do I need for a 10x10 slab? +

A 10 foot by 10 foot slab poured 4 inches thick needs about 33.33 cubic feet, which is 1.23 cubic yards. At 6 inches thick it rises to 1.85 cubic yards. Add a 5–10% waste allowance, so in practice you would order around 1.3–1.4 cubic yards for the 4-inch slab.

How many cubic yards of concrete do I need? +

It depends on your project size. Concrete is ordered by the cubic yard in the US, and one cubic yard is 27 cubic feet. Enter your measurements in the calculator and it returns the volume in cubic yards and cubic meters, with a waste allowance.

How many bags of concrete are in a cubic yard? +

One cubic yard (27 cubic feet) takes about 90 bags of 40 lb concrete, 72 bags of 50 lb, 60 bags of 60 lb, or 45 bags of 80 lb concrete. The calculator shows the bag count for all four sizes at once.

How many 80 lb bags of concrete make a cubic yard? +

It takes 45 bags of 80 lb concrete to make one cubic yard. Each 80 lb bag yields about 0.60 cubic feet of mixed concrete, and 27 cubic feet ÷ 0.60 = 45 bags.

Should I use bagged concrete or ready-mix? +

Bagged concrete is cheaper and more convenient for small jobs below about one cubic yard. Beyond that, mixing by hand becomes impractical — two cubic yards is roughly 90 bags of 80 lb mix — and a ready-mix truck is usually cheaper, stronger, and more consistent. The practical crossover is around one cubic yard.

How much does a cubic yard of concrete weigh? +

Standard concrete weighs about 150 pounds per cubic foot, so one cubic yard weighs roughly 4,050 pounds — just over 2 tons. Reinforced concrete is slightly heavier at about 156 lb per cubic foot, while lightweight concrete is around 110 lb per cubic foot.

How much does a yard of concrete cost? +

Delivered ready-mix concrete is typically priced in the low hundreds of dollars per cubic yard before delivery, and prices vary by region. Expect short-load fees on orders under a full truck. Always get a quote from local plants — the cost estimator on this page accepts your price per yard or per bag.

What is the standard concrete mix ratio? +

A standard general-purpose concrete mix ratio is about 1:2:3 (cement : sand : aggregate), which works out to roughly 17% cement, 33% sand, and 50% aggregate by volume. Higher-strength work uses a richer mix, like 1:1.5:3, while lean footings can run 1:3:6.

Why should I add a waste percentage? +

Adding a waste percentage accounts for spillage, uneven subgrades, over-excavation, and concrete left in the chute. Industry practice is 5–10% for clean pours, rising to 10–15% for rough terrain or irregular shapes. This calculator includes a waste slider so the ordered total updates as you change it.

Can I calculate concrete for columns, walls, and stairs? +

Yes. Pick the shape at the top of the calculator: slab, footing, column (round or square), wall, stairs, curb and gutter, circular pad, or monolithic slab. Walls let you subtract door and window openings, and stairs are figured as solid concrete down to grade.

How thick should a concrete slab be? +

A 4-inch slab handles patios, walkways, garden sheds, and most residential floors. Step up to 5–6 inches for driveways and any surface that will carry vehicles, and reserve 6 inches or more — usually with reinforcement — for heavy vehicles, RV pads, or shop floors.

How long does concrete take to cure? +

Fresh concrete sets within a few hours and is usually safe to walk on after 24–48 hours. It reaches enough strength for light vehicle traffic after about 7 days, and full design strength after about 28 days. Keep the surface damp during the first week.

How accurate is this concrete calculator? +

The calculator uses exact geometry and standard conversion factors, with strict unit handling to prevent the classic inches-as-feet error. Results are reliable for planning and ordering, but always verify measurements on site and consult a professional contractor or engineer for large or structural projects before ordering.
